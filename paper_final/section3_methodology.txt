3 Proposed Methodology
3.1 System Overview
The system takes the raw files produced by the headset and returns one label for
every 10 s task. It works in two stages (Fig. 1). The first stage, signal preparation,
turns a continuous recording into clean, labelled trials: it finds where the task sched-
ule starts, filters the EEG, cuts out the twelve trials, removes the ones spoiled by
artefacts and normalises what is left. The second stage, representation and classifi-
cation, splits each trial into short overlapping windows, describes every window with
spectral, time–frequency, connectivity and covariance features, aligns these features
within each recording and classifies the window with an ensemble of four models. The
predictions for the windows of a trial are then averaged into one decision. Throughout
the pipeline, no participant’s data are ever used for both training and testing.




Fig. 1 Overview of the proposed pipeline.




3.2 Experimental Protocol
Participants sat in front of a screen in a quiet room. They were asked to keep their eyes
open, to follow the instruction shown on the screen, to avoid unnecessary movement
and speech, and to relax whenever a fixation cross appeared. The protocol covers six
movements: right-arm flexion, left-arm flexion, right-leg lifting, left-leg lifting, right-
wrist rotation and left-wrist rotation. Each movement was first carried out physically
(action ) and then rehearsed mentally without moving (imagery ), giving twelve tasks
in a fixed order:

Each task lasted 10 s and was followed by 5 s of rest, so the whole schedule took 175 s
from the start of the first task to the end of the last. Two session layouts occur in the
data (Fig. 2). In most sessions the tasks began a few seconds after the recording was
started. In several later sessions the recording opened with a resting baseline, about
50 s with eyes open followed by about 50 s with eyes closed, so the tasks began roughly
two minutes in. Every trial carries three labels: the task itself (twelve classes), the
condition, action or imagery (two classes), and the body part (six classes).

3.3 Data Acquisition
EEG was recorded with a 14-channel Emotiv EPOC X fitted with saline-soaked felt
electrodes at AF3, AF4, F3, F4, F7, F8, FC5, FC6, T7, T8, P7, P8, O1 and O2, placed
according to the international 10–20 system, with the headset’s CMS/DRL reference.
The signals were sampled at 128 Hz and saved in EDF format by the EmotivPRO soft-
ware. For every session EmotivPRO also saves a second file from the headset’s built-in
inertial sensor, which holds three accelerometer axes and four quaternion (orientation)
components sampled at 32 Hz. We never used this motion file as a classification input;
it served only to locate the task schedule (Section 3.4). In total we collected 59 EEG
recordings containing all fourteen channels from 47 participants. A few participants
were recorded in more than one session.

3.4 Motion-Guided Trial Onset Detection
To cut the trials correctly, we need to know when the first task begins in each recording.
Our first pipeline assumed a fixed delay of 15 s for every file. When we checked this
against the data it turned out to be wrong: the delay between starting the recording
and starting the tasks changed from session to session, and in sessions with a baseline
it was more than two minutes. With a fixed offset, most trials were cut several seconds
early, and a window labelled with one task often contained the end of the previous one.
    To recover the real onset we used a simple physical fact. Moving an arm, a leg
or a wrist shakes the head slightly, and the headset’s motion sensor picks this up;
imagining the same movement does not. The procedure has four steps.

b, the motion energy is the logarithm of the total absolute change across the seven
inertial channels,

3.5 EEG Preprocessing
Preprocessing has three parts: filtering, trial rejection and normalisation (Fig. 4).




Fig. 4 Filtering, trial rejection and per-recording normalisation.



                                                 11
     Band-pass filtering. The EDF files were loaded with MNE-Python [36], and only
the fourteen EEG channels were kept; the quality, battery and marker channels were
discarded and missing samples were set to zero. Each continuous recording was filtered
between 4 and 45 Hz with a zero-phase FIR filter (Hamming-window design, MNE
defaults). The 4 Hz edge removes slow electrode drift and most of the energy of blinks
and eye movements. The 45 Hz edge removes high-frequency muscle activity and the
50 Hz mains interference. What remains covers the theta, mu, beta and low-gamma
rhythms. Because the filter is zero-phase it does not shift the signal in time, and
filtering the whole recording before cutting the trials avoids distortion at the trial
edges.
     Trial extraction. Starting from the detected onset t0 , trial k (k = 0, . . . , 11) runs
from t0 + 15k to t0 + 15k + 10 s. Each trial is therefore a 14 × 1280 matrix, and the
53 recordings give 636 trials.
     Trial rejection. A trial was discarded if it failed any of three checks. (i) It was flat,
with zero variance over the whole trial. (ii) Any sample reached 150 µV. Genuine EEG
on this device stays well below this level (the median peak of a trial was 92 µV), and
larger values nearly always come from blinks, jaw clenching or a moving cable. (iii)
The mean absolute excess kurtosis across channels was 10 or more, which flags sudden
spikes and electrode pops that stay under the amplitude limit. No trial was flat. The
amplitude rule caught 158 trials and the kurtosis rule 29, with 27 caught by both,
so 160 trials (25.2%) were removed. This left 476 clean trials from 51 recordings
and 47 participants, a data array of size 476 × 14 × 1280. Rejection was done before
windowing, so that a noisy trial could not enter the training data as many overlapping
windows. The classes stayed balanced: 235 action and 241 imagery trials, and between
36 and 44 trials for each of the twelve tasks.
     Per-recording normalisation. Signal amplitude changes from session to session with
electrode impedance, the amount of saline and head shape. We therefore z-scored every
channel c of every recording r separately,

3.7 Feature Extraction
Each 2 s window is turned into a fixed-length vector built from five families of features
(Fig. 6): handcrafted spectral, statistical, fractal and wavelet measures; a spectrogram;
a scalogram; inter-hemispheric connectivity; and the spatial covariance of the fourteen
channels. The first four families give 1,666 values and the covariance family gives 105,
for 1,771 features per window. None of them uses class labels.

3.7.4 Connectivity and Riemannian Covariance Features
Inter-hemispheric connectivity (56 features). Moving one side of the body mainly
engages the opposite hemisphere. The fourteen electrodes form seven left–right pairs
(AF3–AF4, F3–F4, F7–F8, FC5–FC6, T7–T8, P7–P8 and O1–O2). For each pair and
each of the four bands we compute the magnitude-squared coherence and the power
asymmetry log(Pleft /Pright ), which gives 7 × 4 × 2 = 56 features.

3.9 Hemispheric Mirror Augmentation
The electrodes of the EPOC X are arranged in symmetric pairs. Since a limb on one
side of the body is controlled mainly by the opposite hemisphere, swapping each left
electrode with its right partner gives a rough picture of the same movement made on
the other side. We use this to add a mirrored copy of every training window (Fig. 9).
Because every feature is computed per channel or per channel pair, the mirrored
features can be built directly. The handcrafted, spectrogram and scalogram columns of
partner electrodes are swapped, coherence stays the same, the sign of the asymmetry
is flipped, and the re-centred covariance matrix is permuted, C̃ ′ = P C̃P ⊤ , before it
is projected to the tangent space. The labels are mirrored in the same way (right arm
becomes left arm, right leg becomes left leg, right wrist becomes left wrist), while the
action or imagery condition is kept. Mirrored copies are added to the training folds
only; test windows are never altered.

3.11 Imagery-versus-Imagery (Left versus Right) Task
The imagery-versus-imagery binary task asks whether the side of an imagined move-
ment can be decoded. Only the 241 imagery trials are used, and the target is the
side of the body, left or right, pooled over arm, leg and wrist. For this task the per-
recording standardisation of Section 3.8 is computed from that recording’s imagery
windows only (three left and three right trials) rather than from all twelve tasks; the
side labels are not used. Mirror augmentation is applied as before, with the side label
flipped. As a reference we also ran the standard method for this kind of problem,
filter-bank CSP followed by shrinkage LDA [20], and all eight classifiers listed above.
Significance against the 50% chance level was tested with a one-sided binomial test.

All windows of a trial always belong to the same test fold, so this averaging never
mixes training and test data. Window-level accuracy is reported as well.
    Person-wise GroupKFold. The main results use GroupKFold cross-validation
grouped by participant (Fig. 10a). The participants are divided into folds, and each
of them is tested exactly once, by a model that never saw any of their data. Five folds
were used for the two binary tasks (9–10 test participants per fold) and three folds for
the six- and twelve-class tasks (15–16 test participants per fold), so that every class
keeps enough training examples. Folds are grouped by person rather than by record-
ing, so a participant with two sessions can never appear on both sides of a split. The
neural networks and the hierarchical model used three folds for all tasks.
    Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO). As a stricter check, the ensemble and its four mem-
bers were also evaluated with leave-one-subject-out validation (Fig. 10b). Each of the
47 participants is held out in turn, and a new model is trained on the other 46, giving
47 folds per task. The label-free steps are unchanged, and the scaler, the classifiers and

    Validity checks. Several further checks were run on the final pipeline. (1) Label per-
mutation : the labels were shuffled across trials within each recording and the complete
pipeline, including alignment, mirror augmentation, the ensemble and trial averaging,
was run again; this was done three times for every task, twenty more times for the
binary task, and five times under LOSO (four runs for the binary task and one for
the imagery-versus-imagery task). (2) Reproducibility : the pipelines were rerun with
the same random seed, and the binary pipeline also with three other seeds. (3) Learn-
ing curve : for the binary task, the same test participants were classified by models
trained on 5, 10, 20 and 30 randomly chosen training participants and on all of them.
(4) Nested cross-validation : inside each outer training set, an inner person-wise cross-
validation chose the configuration (no alignment, alignment, or alignment with mirror
augmentation; for the imagery-versus-imagery task, standard or imagery-only calibra-
tion with or without mirroring) and the classifier, and only that choice was tested on
the outer test participants. This removes the optimism that comes from comparing
several set-ups on the same test people. (5) Onset sensitivity : every detected onset
was shifted by ±2 s and the classification was repeated.
    Metrics. Accuracy is reported as the number of correctly classified test trials pooled
over all folds, and as the mean ± standard deviation across folds. For the LOSO results
we also report balanced accuracy, macro-averaged F1, Cohen’s kappa, the accuracy
per participant, and the accuracy on the training participants, which shows the gap
between fit and generalisation. Chance level is 50% for the binary tasks, 16.67% for
six classes and 8.33% for twelve classes. Accuracies were compared with chance using
