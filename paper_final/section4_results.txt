the log-variance of a spatially filtered signal, which is a weighted combination of band
power and inter-channel covariance; our feature set already contains band power for
every channel in every band and the full covariance matrix in tangent space, so most
of what CSP extracts is already present. Second, CSP filters are learned from the
training participants, and spatial patterns transfer poorly between people when only
14 electrodes are available and none of them lies over the central motor strip.

4.8 Most Informative Features
To see which features separate action from imagery, an ANOVA F -score was computed
for every feature on the training participants of each fold and averaged over the five
folds (Fig. 13).




Fig. 13 Discriminative power of the features for action versus imagery: (a) the 15 features with the
highest ANOVA F -score; (b) median F -score of each feature family.


    Almost all of the strongest features are mu-band measures: relative mu power, the
mu-to-theta ratio and the sensorimotor ratio. This agrees with the spectrogram and
scalogram maps, in which imagery trials carried more mu power than action trials, the
expected sign of stronger desynchronisation during real movement [3]. A scalogram
feature, the temporal variance of the 12 Hz wavelet energy at F3, also appears among
the strongest. By family, the handcrafted features have the highest median F -score
(43.0), followed by the scalogram (31.5), the spectrogram (13.7) and connectivity (7.8).
    One caveat is important. The strongest features come from the frontal electrodes
AF3, AF4, F3 and F4, not from the central motor cortex, which the EPOC X does not


                                                27
cover. Frontal sites are also the most exposed to eye movement and muscle activity,
and Figs. 7 and 8 show extra low-frequency power at AF3 and extra high-frequency
power at FC5 and T8 during action trials. Part of the action-versus-imagery accuracy
is therefore probably due to the physical side effects of moving, such as eye, head
and muscle activity, rather than to cortical activity alone. Separating the two would
require ICA-based artefact removal or EMG reference channels.

4.9 Error Analysis
Fig. 14 shows the confusion matrices of the proposed system, pooled over all test folds.

part; (c) twelve tasks.


    On the binary task the errors were fairly balanced: 77.0% of action trials and 83.4%
of imagery trials were recognised. For body part, the classifier was better at telling
which limb than which side. The arm, leg or wrist was identified correctly in 46.0%
of trials (chance 33.3%), but the side in only 54.6% (chance 50%). Right arm (37.8%)

strongest over C3 and C4, where the EPOC X has no electrodes. In the twelve-task
problem, the action-or-imagery part of the label was correct in 78.2% of trials, but
the body part in only 25.8%, so most twelve-task errors come from the body part and
not from the condition.

4.11 Validity of the Results
Several checks were run to make sure the results are not caused by information leaking
from the test participants (Table 13).
    Fold integrity. In every fold of every task, under both GroupKFold and LOSO,
no participant, recording or trial appeared in both the training and the test set, and
every trial was tested exactly once.
    Label permutation. A pipeline that leaks information keeps scoring above chance
even when the labels are meaningless. Ours did not: with shuffled labels, all four tasks
fell to the level expected from guessing. For action versus imagery, twenty further
shuffles ranged from 41.8% to 52.9%, and the real result lies about 11 standard devi-
ations above their mean. The spread of these shuffled scores is wider than a simple
binomial model would predict, because trials of the same person are correlated; the
highest shuffled LOSO score (56.72%) lies within this spread.

4.12 Limitations
Three limitations matter most. First, every participant performed the tasks in the
same order, so slow drift or fatigue during a session may be partly confounded with
the task label, particularly for the imagery-versus-imagery task. Second, artefacts were
handled by filtering and trial rejection only; eye and muscle activity may therefore
contribute to the action-versus-imagery result, and ICA-based artefact removal would
help to separate it from cortical activity. Third, all results are offline, and subject-
wise alignment assumes one unlabelled calibration session for each new user; an online
evaluation with real-time feedback remains to be done.
